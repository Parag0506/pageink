Notes on paper

Paper was made from rags for a long time. The pulp was beaten in water, lifted on a mould and pressed flat.

Paper and ink share a long history across many lands and ages, and the marker words stoneware quokka teapot sit late in this sentence, so a stroke over them has a clear place to stay when the page moves, reloads or turns.

Iron gall ink was the standard writing ink in Europe for over a thousand years. It bites into the paper.

Pens changed the shape of letters. A broad nib held at a steady angle makes thick and thin strokes.

Pencils came later. Graphite from Borrowdale was so pure that it could be sawn into sticks.

Parchment is animal skin, scraped thin and dried under tension. It takes ink well and lasts.

Vellum is the finest parchment, made from calfskin. Medieval books used it for their best pages.

Blotting paper soaks up wet ink. Before it, writers dusted the page with fine sand.

Fountain pens hold their own ink. The feed lets air in as ink flows out, so the line stays even.

Ballpoint pens roll a tiny ball through thick, oily ink. They write on almost anything.

Typewriters arrived in the 1870s. The keyboard layout of that time is still in use today.

Copying presses made a duplicate of a letter by pressing damp tissue against fresh ink.

Carbon paper replaced the press. A sheet between two pages copied every keystroke.
