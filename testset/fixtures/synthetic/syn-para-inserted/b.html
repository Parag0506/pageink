A Short History of Coastal Lights

Origins of the coastal light

This account draws on harbour records, keepers logs and the recollections of families who lived at the stations. Dates are given where the records agree and left out where they do not, and readers should treat the early chapters as a sketch rather than a full history.

Long before engineers drew plans for stone towers, fishing villages kept a fire burning on the highest rock above the harbour. A boy or an old sailor fed the flames through the night, and boats returning late steered by the glow. The custom was practical rather than grand, and most of these early beacons left no trace beyond a blackened patch of ground.

The first purpose-built towers appeared where trade routes crowded into narrow channels. Merchants paid a small toll for each cargo that passed, and the money bought timber, coal and the wages of a keeper. Records from several ports show that the toll was resented in good years and gladly paid after a winter of wrecks.

Coal fires gave way to oil lamps once glass could be made clear enough to shelter a flame from the wind. An oil lamp burned more steadily and needed less tending, which allowed one keeper to manage a light alone. The change also moved the flame indoors, behind panes that salt spray quickly dulled.

Lenses and reflectors

A bare flame sends light in every direction, and most of it is wasted on the sky and the land behind the tower. Polished metal reflectors were the first answer. A curved dish placed behind each lamp gathered the stray light and threw it out toward the sea in a tighter beam.

The stepped lens changed everything. By cutting a large lens into rings of glass, each ring bending light by the right amount, makers produced an optic that was thin, light and remarkably bright. A single lamp behind such a lens could be seen from distances that had needed a dozen lamps before.

Lenses were graded by size into orders, the largest standing taller than a person and weighing several tonnes. Smaller orders served harbour entrances and river mouths, where a ship needed to see the light only for the last few miles of its approach.

The life of a keeper

Keeping a light was steady, lonely work. The lamp had to be lit at sunset and put out at dawn, the wick trimmed through the night, and the glass cleaned every morning. Many keepers wrote a short entry in the log at each change of watch, noting the wind, the sea and any vessel in sight.

The glass must be cleaned before breakfast, for a dirty lens is a dark lens, and a dark lens is no light at all.

  • Light the lamp at sunset and record the time.
  • Trim the wick every four hours through the night.
  • Wind the clockwork that turns the lens.
  • Clean the glass and polish the brass each morning.
  • Report any wreck or vessel in distress at once.

Families often lived at the station, and children learned to read the weather before they learned their letters. Supplies came by boat when the sea allowed, which on exposed rocks could mean weeks of waiting. Stations on the mainland were easier, with a garden, a cow and neighbours within a day of walking.

Automation and the end of the keepers

Electric lamps arrived at the larger stations first, followed by switches that turned the light on when daylight faded. Each improvement removed one of the duties in the keeper list, and by the middle of the last century a single technician could look after several lights from a shore base.

The last resident keepers left their towers within living memory. Some stations were sold as houses or turned into small museums, while others stand empty with only a modern lamp on the gallery. Ships now carry satellite receivers, yet many captains still say that nothing reassures like a familiar light coming over the horizon.

Preservation groups now raise money to repaint towers and repair lens rooms. Their volunteers guide visitors up the spiral stairs and explain how the clockwork once turned a tonne of glass with a weight no heavier than a bucket of sand.
