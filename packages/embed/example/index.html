A short lesson

Why the tide comes twice a day

The Moon pulls on the whole Earth, but not evenly. That small difference is enough to lift the oceans on two sides at once.

Two bulges, not one

Gravity weakens with distance. The ocean on the side of the Earth facing the Moon is pulled a little harder than the Earth's centre, so it bulges toward the Moon.

The ocean on the far side is pulled a little less than the centre. The Earth is drawn away from that water, and it is left behind as a second bulge. What matters is the difference in pull across the planet, not the pull itself.

Why twice a day

The Earth turns under both bulges once a day. A harbour passes through the near bulge, then the far one, so it sees two high tides and two low tides.

The Moon also moves along its orbit while the Earth turns, so the harbour needs about 50 more minutes to catch up with it. That is why high tide is a little later each day.

Work it out

The tidal pull falls with the cube of the distance. A few lines of code show how much stronger the Moon's effect is than the Sun's, even though the Sun pulls harder overall.

JavaScript 
// Tidal effect is mass over distance cubed.
const moon = { mass: 7.35e22, dist: 3.84e8 };
const sun  = { mass: 1.99e30, dist: 1.50e11 };

const tidal = (b) => b.mass / b.dist ** 3;

const ratio = tidal(moon) / tidal(sun);
console.log(ratio.toFixed(2)); // about 2.2

// Spring tides: Sun and Moon in line, effects add.
const spring = tidal(moon) + tidal(sun);
// Neap tides: at right angles, they partly cancel.
const neap = tidal(moon) - tidal(sun);
console.log((spring / neap).toFixed(2)); // about 2.7

Spring and neap tides

Twice a month the Sun, Earth and Moon line up, and the Sun's smaller pull adds to the Moon's. These are spring tides, with the highest highs and the lowest lows. A week later the two pulls are at right angles and the range is smallest. These are neap tides.
