Opening words of the long article inside its own scrolling pane.

Paper was made from rags for a long time. The pulp was beaten in water, lifted on a mould and pressed flat.

Iron gall ink was the standard writing ink in Europe for over a thousand years.

A broad nib held at a steady angle makes thick and thin strokes without any effort from the writer.

Graphite from Borrowdale was so pure that it could be sawn into sticks and wrapped in wood.

Quills were cut from the flight feathers of geese and swans, and a good scribe recut them often.

Blotting paper soaked up the extra ink before the page was turned.

Sealing wax kept letters private on their way across the country.

The fountain pen carried its own ink and made the inkwell unnecessary.

Ballpoints came last, with a tiny rolling ball and thick oily ink.

Typewriters arrived and the hand fell out of use in offices for a while.

Calligraphy kept the old skills alive in schools and studios.

Some writers still draft every book by hand in the morning.

The closing paragraph mentions an albatross so the far end has a target.
